## Title
Distributed Heat-Vulnerability Mapping for Mid-Sized Municipal Adaptation Planning

## PI/Institution
Dr. Miriam T. Halsey, Department of Urban and Regional Planning, Blackwood State University

## Abstract
Mid-sized U.S. cities (population 50,000–250,000) face growing exposure to
extreme heat events but typically lack the analytic capacity of larger
metropolitan planning offices. This project develops and pilots a low-cost,
sensor-informed heat-vulnerability mapping toolkit designed for adoption by
municipal planning departments with a single GIS staff member. Working in
partnership with three participating cities in the mid-Atlantic region, we
will (a) deploy a distributed network of low-cost temperature loggers over
two summer seasons, (b) integrate the resulting microclimate data with
existing parcel- and block-level vulnerability indicators, and (c) produce
a reproducible workflow, published as open documentation, for planners to
replicate the method locally.

## Aims
- **Aim 1:** Instrument three partner cities with a low-cost distributed
  heat-logger network (approximately 40 loggers per city) and collect two
  summer seasons of microclimate data.
- **Aim 2:** Develop an open-source workflow that fuses logger data with
  parcel-level vulnerability indicators (age, income, tree canopy,
  building age) to produce block-level heat-risk classifications.
- **Aim 3:** Deliver replication documentation and hold a two-day
  practitioner workshop for planners from at least fifteen additional
  mid-sized municipalities.

## Background & Prior Work
The public health consequences of urban heat are well established, and
recent work has documented substantial within-city variation in surface
and near-surface temperatures at the neighborhood scale [1]. Existing
national-scale vulnerability indices, however, are typically produced at
census-tract resolution and mask the parcel- and block-level variation
that matters most for planning interventions such as tree planting,
cool-roof incentives, and cooling-center siting.

Recent methodological work has demonstrated that dense networks of
low-cost temperature loggers can capture microclimate variation at
spatial resolutions unavailable from municipal weather stations alone.
As Fenwick and Ortega-Bailey note, "practitioner-grade sensor networks
now offer resolution comparable to research deployments at less than a
tenth of the per-unit cost" [2]. Parallel work on community-partnered
data collection has shown that sensor placement informed by local
knowledge outperforms grid-based placement in identifying heat-exposed
populations [3]. What remains missing is an integrated, documented
workflow that a two-person planning office can actually execute — a
gap this project directly addresses.

## Approach
We will partner with the planning departments of three participating
mid-Atlantic cities to deploy roughly 40 loggers per city across a
stratified sample of block groups, prioritizing areas identified by
existing tract-level vulnerability screens. Loggers will be affixed to
municipal light poles under standard right-of-way permits and read out
monthly. Fusion of logger data with parcel-level indicators will follow
the general framework of [2], with modifications for the smaller
municipal context. In a pilot deployment during summer 2025, a
preliminary version of the workflow identified block-level heat-risk
classifications that were 41% more spatially concordant with recorded
EMS heat-illness calls than the census-tract baseline, suggesting the
approach captures actionable within-tract variation.
